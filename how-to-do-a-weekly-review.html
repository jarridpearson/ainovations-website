Weekly Review Guide

How to do a weekly review that actually improves your decisions.

A useful weekly review is not a list of everything you completed. It is a structured check on whether your time, decisions, and actions are supporting the priorities that matter most.

The purpose of a weekly review

A weekly review creates a regular pause between activity and direction. It helps you see what changed, what stalled, which decisions had consequences, and where attention should go next.

Without a review, it is easy to confuse motion with progress. Tasks get completed, messages get answered, and urgent problems consume time while important priorities remain unchanged.

The goal is not to judge whether the week felt productive. The goal is to compare what happened against the priorities and outcomes you intended to support.

1. Gather the week’s evidence

Begin with information rather than memory alone. Review the records that show how the week was actually spent.

  • Priority progress indicators.
  • Important decisions made during the week.
  • Completed and unfinished actions.
  • Calendar commitments.
  • Unexpected problems or opportunities.
  • Notes about changes in circumstances.

2. Review each priority separately

Look at every active priority and ask whether it improved, declined, or remained largely unchanged.

For each priority, answer:

  • What evidence of progress exists?
  • What action contributed most?
  • What blocked movement?
  • Did another priority receive attention at its expense?
  • Does the current indicator still represent meaningful progress?

3. Examine important decisions

Weekly reviews are especially valuable when decisions are recorded while the reasoning is still fresh.

Ask:

  • Which decisions created immediate effects?
  • Which decisions require more time before evaluation?
  • What assumptions influenced the choice?
  • Did the decision support the intended priority?
  • What risks or tradeoffs need monitoring?

4. Look for patterns, not isolated moments

One difficult day may not mean the system is failing. Repeated delays, recurring conflicts, and the same unfinished action can reveal a pattern that deserves attention.

Patterns may appear in when work gets completed, which obligations are repeatedly postponed, how often urgent requests interrupt planned work, or where progress depends on another person or system.

5. Identify conflicts between priorities

Priorities compete for limited time, energy, money, and attention. A weekly review should make those conflicts visible rather than pretending every priority can advance equally.

Decide whether the conflict was intentional, temporary, or evidence that expectations need to change.

6. Evaluate unfinished work honestly

Unfinished work is not automatically failure. It may reveal that the task was poorly defined, dependent on missing information, lower in value than expected, or unrealistic within the available time.

For every important unfinished action, choose one response:

  1. Complete it next week.
  2. Break it into a smaller action.
  3. Delegate it.
  4. Delay it intentionally.
  5. Remove it because it no longer supports a priority.

7. Choose the next meaningful actions

End the review by choosing a limited number of actions that can produce meaningful movement during the next week.

Each action should connect clearly to a priority, resolve an identified obstacle, test an assumption, or move an important decision forward.

Weekly review questions

  1. What changed this week?
  2. Which priority received the most attention?
  3. Which priority received too little attention?
  4. What evidence shows real progress?
  5. What important decision was made?
  6. What tradeoff did that decision create?
  7. What repeatedly blocked progress?
  8. What should stop, continue, or change?
  9. What are the most important next actions?
  10. What should be reviewed again next week?

What to avoid

  • Turning the review into a complete diary of the week.
  • Measuring activity that does not reveal meaningful progress.
  • Adding more tasks without removing lower-value work.
  • Changing priorities whenever progress feels uncomfortable.
  • Ignoring context behind temporary changes.
  • Using AI-generated conclusions as replacements for judgment.
