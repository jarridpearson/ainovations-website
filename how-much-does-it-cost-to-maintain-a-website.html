How Much Does It Cost to Maintain a Website?

Almost every small business asks what a website costs to build. Far fewer ask what it costs to keep, which is unfortunate, because the second number is the one that goes on repeating for the next ten years.

Here is what is actually on the bill after launch.

The line items nobody mentions in the quote

  • Hosting. The server your site lives on. Cheap at the bottom, and the cheap tiers are cheap for reasons you discover during your busiest week.
  • The domain name. Small, annual, and catastrophic the one year the renewal card expires and nobody notices.
  • SSL certificate. The padlock. Often free now, not always, and a lapsed one puts a browser warning in front of every visitor.
  • Platform and plugin updates. If your site runs on WordPress or anything like it, the software underneath it changes constantly. Skipping updates is how sites get defaced; applying them carelessly is how sites break.
  • Backups. Worth nothing until the single day they are worth everything.
  • Security monitoring. Small business sites are not targeted personally. They are swept up automatically, which is worse, because nobody is watching.
  • Analytics. Free to run. Not free to read, if you want it read by someone who will tell you what it means.
  • Content. The big one. A site nobody touches gets stale, and stale is visible to customers long before it is visible anywhere else.

Why the real cost is hours, not dollars

Add the subscriptions above and you get a number that looks manageable. Then add the part that is not a subscription: somebody has to actually do it. Somebody has to notice the contact form stopped sending, update the hours before the holiday, replace the photo of the truck you sold, and apply the update that patches the vulnerability announced on Tuesday.

In a small business, that somebody is usually the owner, at nine at night, badly, six months late. That is the true maintenance cost and it does not appear on any invoice.

Three ways this usually goes

  • Do it yourself. Lowest cash cost, highest time cost, and the one most likely to quietly stop happening by month four.
  • Pay hourly when something breaks. Feels thrifty. In practice you only call when it is already broken, so you are buying emergencies at emergency prices and never buying the maintenance that would have prevented them.
  • Pay a flat monthly fee. Predictable, and the work happens whether or not you remember to ask for it.
