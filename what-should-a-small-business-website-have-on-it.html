What Should a Small Business Website Actually Have on It?

Most quotes start from a page count, which is the wrong unit entirely. Nobody has ever chosen a contractor because the site had eleven pages. What decides it is whether a stranger, on a phone, in about ten seconds, can answer four questions.

The four questions

  • What exactly do you do? Not "solutions." The actual work: septic pumping, collision repair, well pumps, dumpster rental.
  • Do you serve me? Towns, counties, or a radius. This is the single most common omission we see, and it is the one that loses the most calls — people will not call to find out whether you drive that far.
  • Roughly what does it cost? You do not have to publish a full price list. A range, a minimum, a "most jobs run between" is enough to stop the tire-kickers and reassure everyone else.
  • How do I get hold of you, right now? A phone number that dials when tapped, and a form for people who will not call.

A site that answers those four is already better than most of its competition. Everything after that is improvement, not necessity.

What earns its place

  • Your hours, including the holiday ones. Wrong hours are worse than no hours.
  • Photos of your own work. Ten real job photos from a phone beat any stock library. People are checking whether you are real.
  • Proof. Licenses, insurance, years in business, a certificate of insurance people can download if your trade needs it.
  • One obvious next action per page. Call, book, request a quote. One. Two choices halves each of them.
  • Words. Search engines read text, not pictures. A page whose content is baked into an image is invisible — which is one reason businesses find they are not showing up on Google.

What almost never gets read

The About Us page about your passion. The mission statement. The stock photo of a handshake. A blog with two posts from 2022, which actively signals the business might be gone. A carousel — people scroll past before slide two. None of it is harmful exactly; it is just budget spent where nobody is looking.

So how many pages is that?

For most small shops, about five: home, the services you actually want more of, service area, proof, and contact. If your trades are genuinely different businesses — excavation and dumpster rental, say — each wants its own page, because people search for them separately. Past about fifteen pages you are usually in catalog territory, which is a different build.

The honest test is not length. It is whether a stranger could call you from their truck, in a parking lot, without pinch-zooming. If they can, the site is doing its job.
