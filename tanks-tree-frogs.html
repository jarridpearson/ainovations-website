Tree & Terrestrial Frog Setup: Humidity, Not Water Chemistry

Tree and terrestrial frogs live on land, not underwater — so forget pH and nitrate and think like a terrarium keeper. What matters here is humidity, temperature, and gentle UVB, plus the right layout: tree frogs want a tall, planted, vertical terrarium to climb, while the ground-dwelling Pacman frog wants moist substrate to burrow into and must be kept alone. Most of these are gorgeous look-don't-handle animals.

This guide covers the difference between a climbing tree frog and a burrowing Pacman frog, the humidity and temperature each wants, why these are land animals that don't use water chemistry, and who you can (and can't) keep together.

First: Climber or Burrower?

Tree frogs (climbers). The hardy White's Tree Frog (Dumpy), the small American Green Tree Frog, and the iconic Red-Eyed Tree Frog are arboreal — they want a tall, planted vertical terrarium with branches and foliage to climb and a shallow water dish. White's is a great, forgiving beginner frog (just don't overfeed it — they're prone to obesity). The red-eyed tree frog is beautiful but delicate and needs high humidity, so it's a step up, not a first frog.

Pacman frog (burrower). The Pacman Frog (Horned Frog) is a ground-dwelling ambush predator that buries itself in moist substrate and eats anything it can fit in its mouth. Keep exactly one per enclosure — they are cannibalistic — and never with tankmates. It mostly sits still and is not a handling pet.

The Essentials: Humidity, Temperature, and Gentle UVB

These are land animals. A healthy setup tracks air temperature, humidity, and UVB — not aquarium water chemistry like pH, ammonia, or nitrate. The targets vary by species:

  • White's Tree Frog: ~72–82°F, humidity ~50–70%, a tall planted terrarium with branches and a shallow water dish. Low-level UVB is beneficial. Don't overfeed.
  • American Green Tree Frog: ~70–85°F, humidity ~50–70%, a vertical planted terrarium. Nocturnal and delicate.
  • Red-Eyed Tree Frog: high humidity ~70–90%, ~75–85°F days with a slight night drop, dense foliage, and gentle UVB. Keep airflow up to avoid skin infections.
  • Pacman Frog: moist substrate to burrow in, ~75–82°F, humidity ~60–80%. Ground setup, no climbing needed.

A shallow water dish, not a pond. Tree frogs need a shallow dish of clean, dechlorinated water to sit in — their permeable skin absorbs whatever is in it — but they are not swimmers and don't need deep water.

Who Can Live Together?

As a rule, keep these frogs with their own kind or alone. A Pacman frog must be solo — it will eat tankmates and even other Pacman frogs. Tree frogs can sometimes be kept in same-species groups of similar size in a large enough terrarium, but mixing species or sizes invites bullying, disease transfer, and one frog eating another. These are also look-don't-handle animals: frequent handling stresses them and their skin is easily harmed.

Tree & Terrestrial Frog Care FAQ

Do tree frogs need water parameters like pH and nitrate?

No. Tree and terrestrial frogs are land animals, so you track air temperature, humidity, and UVB — not aquarium water chemistry. They only need a shallow dish of clean, dechlorinated water to sit in, because their skin absorbs whatever is in it.

What's the best beginner tree frog?

The White's Tree Frog (Dumpy) is the usual recommendation — hardy, forgiving, and long-lived. It wants a tall planted terrarium around 72–82°F with 50–70% humidity. Just don't overfeed it; they're prone to obesity. The red-eyed tree frog is beautiful but delicate and better for a second frog.

Can you keep more than one Pacman frog together?

No. Pacman frogs are cannibalistic ambush predators — keep exactly one per enclosure and never with any tankmates. They bury themselves in moist substrate, eat anything they can fit in their mouth, and are a look-don't-handle animal.

What humidity do tree frogs need?

It depends on the species. White's and American green tree frogs want roughly 50–70% humidity; the red-eyed tree frog wants high humidity around 70–90% with good airflow to prevent skin infections. Matching humidity to the species is the key to keeping them healthy.

Can I handle my tree frog?

Best to minimize it. Tree and terrestrial frogs are look-don't-handle animals — their permeable skin is easily harmed and frequent handling stresses them. Enjoy watching them and handle only when necessary, with clean, wet hands.
