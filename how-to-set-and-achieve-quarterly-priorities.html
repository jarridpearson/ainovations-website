Priorities & Progress

How to set quarterly priorities and actually achieve them.

Most quarterly goals aren't wrong — they're just forgotten by week three. The fix isn't a better planning session. It's the rhythm that runs between sessions.

Everward · Priorities & progress · 7 min read

If you run on EOS, Scaling Up, or your own version of quarterly planning, you already know the pattern: the offsite feels great, three or four "rocks" get set, and by mid-quarter half of them have quietly stalled. The planning was fine. What broke was everything that happened after.

1. Pick few, and make them real priorities

A priority is something you're willing to protect at the expense of other things. If everything is a priority, nothing is. Cap the quarter at three to five priorities per person. More than that and you don't have priorities — you have a wish list that competes with itself.

2. Write outcomes, not activities

"Work on the onboarding flow" is an activity — it's never done and never measurable. "Cut new-user setup time from 20 minutes to under 5" is an outcome. Every priority should have a number and a date attached, so at quarter end there's no debate about whether you hit it.

3. Connect daily decisions to the priority

Priorities don't move on their own; they move through the decisions you make each week. Before you commit time or money to something, the useful question is simply: which of my priorities does this serve — and is it the highest one? Tracking the decision, not just the task, is what later tells you whether your judgment was any good.

4. Run a weekly review — this is the whole game

The single biggest predictor of hitting quarterly goals isn't the goal itself; it's whether there's a short, honest weekly check on it. Ten minutes a week: Is each priority ahead, on track, or slipping? What decision is due? What's blocked? A quarter is really twelve of these reviews in a row. Skip them and you're back to remembering your goals in month three.

5. Catch priority conflicts before they surface

The quiet killer of a quarter is two priorities competing for the same time, budget, or person — a structural conflict nobody named at planning. "Ship the new feature" and "cut spend 15%" can both be reasonable and still be pulling against each other. Naming that tension early turns a mid-quarter crisis into a five-minute tradeoff conversation.

6. Score your decisions, not just your outcomes

At quarter end, most people grade the result and move on. The more useful review asks: were you calibrated? Where were you overconfident, where too cautious, and in which kinds of decisions? Over a few quarters that pattern — not any single outcome — is what actually improves your judgment.

7. Close the loop and reset

End the quarter by comparing what you set against what happened, keep what worked, and carry the honest lessons into the next planning session. Done consistently, each quarter compounds on the last instead of starting from a blank page.
