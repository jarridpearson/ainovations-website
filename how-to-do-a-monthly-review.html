Monthly Review Guide

How to do a monthly review that reveals real progress.

A monthly review looks beyond individual tasks and isolated days. It helps you evaluate whether your priorities, decisions, habits, and use of resources are moving your life or organization in the intended direction.

Why a monthly review is different

A weekly review focuses on immediate actions, recent decisions, and short-term obstacles. A monthly review examines broader patterns.

It creates enough distance to see whether repeated actions are producing meaningful results or simply maintaining activity.

The purpose is not to assign a grade to the month. It is to understand what changed, why it changed, and what should happen next.

1. Gather the month’s evidence

Review the information connected to each active priority before drawing conclusions.

Useful evidence includes progress indicators, important decisions, weekly review notes, completed milestones, financial information, calendar commitments, and changes in circumstances.

Do not rely only on what felt most memorable. Recent events can distort how the entire month is judged.

2. Evaluate each priority

Review every priority separately and compare its current condition with the beginning of the month.

Identify where measurable progress occurred, where conditions declined, and where little meaningful change happened.

Ask whether the priority received the time, attention, money, and decisions it required.

3. Review important decisions

Examine the major decisions made during the month and the reasoning recorded at the time.

Determine which assumptions proved accurate, which risks appeared, and which consequences remain too early to evaluate.

A decision should be reviewed against the information available when it was made, not only against the final result.

4. Identify repeated patterns

Monthly reviews are especially useful for recognizing patterns that are difficult to see within a single week.

Look for recurring delays, repeated priority conflicts, frequent changes of direction, avoidable interruptions, and decisions that repeatedly produce the same consequence.

A repeated pattern deserves a system-level response rather than another promise to try harder.

5. Make tradeoffs visible

Progress in one priority may have required less attention somewhere else.

Identify which tradeoffs were intentional and which happened without a deliberate choice.

The goal is not equal progress everywhere. The goal is alignment between what you said mattered and how resources were actually used.

6. Decide what should change

Choose a small number of changes based on the evidence.

You may need to change an indicator, remove a low-value action, resolve a repeated obstacle, narrow a goal, or make a postponed decision.

Avoid rebuilding the entire system every month. Preserve what is working and change what the evidence shows is not working.

Monthly review questions

Which priority improved most?

Which priority received too little attention?

What decision had the largest effect?

What pattern repeated throughout the month?

What tradeoff was accepted?

What evidence contradicts the current plan?

What should continue, stop, or change next month?
