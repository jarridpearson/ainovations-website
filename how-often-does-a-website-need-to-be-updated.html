How Often Does a Website Need to Be Updated?

People argue past each other on this one, because two completely different jobs share the word. One is content — the words and prices and photos. The other is software — the platform the site runs on. Content going stale costs you customers slowly. Software going stale gets you hacked.

The software side: quarterly at the outside, monthly is better

Whatever your site is built on has updates, and most of them are security patches. Skip them long enough and you are not running an old website, you are running a known vulnerability with your logo on it. What that actually looks like when it goes wrong:

  • Your site starts serving spam pages you cannot see, usually about pharmaceuticals or casinos.
  • Google flags it, and visitors get a red interstitial warning them away. That is worse than being down.
  • Your host suspends the account, and now the site is down.
  • If you take any customer information at all, you are into notification obligations, which is a lawyer's problem rather than a web problem.

None of that is dramatic or rare. It is the ordinary fate of a site nobody has logged into for two years. If your arrangement does not include somebody doing this, it is being done by nobody — which is worth knowing before you compare monthly prices, because this is most of what maintenance actually buys.

The content side: the day it changes

A short list has to be right the day it changes, not at the next review:

  • Hours, and especially holiday hours. Wrong hours send someone to a locked door, and that person tells people.
  • Prices, if you publish them. A stale price is a customer argument you are going to lose.
  • Phone number and service area.
  • Seasonal anything. A winterizing page still up in June reads as abandoned.
  • Staff who have left, particularly if customers ask for them by name.

This is the part that should never require booking a developer. If changing your own hours means emailing somebody and waiting, that is a flaw in the arrangement, not a fact of life.

And adding new content: honestly, only if you want to gain ground

Publishing something new every month or so is how a site keeps earning search traffic. One useful page a month beats four rushed ones, and beats a blog that gets abandoned in March — a dead blog is a worse signal than no blog.

But let us be straight: a site you never add to still works. It still answers the phone-number question, still shows up for your name, still closes the sale a referral already half-made. It just stops climbing. If you are booked solid and happy, that may be exactly the right amount of effort — a case we made in full for shops that do not want more phone calls.

A schedule you could actually keep

  • Same day: hours, prices, phone, service area, staff changes.
  • Monthly: platform and security updates; check the site still loads on a phone; glance at whether anything looks broken.
  • Monthly-ish, optional: one new page or post, if growth matters to you.
  • Yearly: re-read every page as a stranger. Things quietly stop being true.
