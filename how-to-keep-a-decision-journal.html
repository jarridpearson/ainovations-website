Decision Journal Guide

How to keep a decision journal and learn from your choices.

A decision journal preserves what you knew, believed, expected, and valued at the time of a choice. It creates a record that can later be reviewed without relying on memory or rewriting the past.

Why a decision journal is useful

Memory changes after an outcome becomes known.

Successful results can make weak reasoning look better than it was, while disappointing results can make responsible reasoning appear foolish.

A decision journal preserves the original context so the quality of the process can be reviewed separately from luck.

1. Record the decision clearly

Describe the choice in one direct sentence.

Avoid vague entries such as deciding what to do about work. State the actual alternatives and what must be chosen.

Include the date and any deadline affecting the decision.

2. Connect the decision to priorities

Identify which priorities the decision is intended to support.

Also record any priority that may be negatively affected.

This makes the tradeoff visible and gives the decision a standard against which it can later be evaluated.

3. Separate facts from assumptions

List the information known to be true and distinguish it from beliefs, estimates, and predictions.

Record where the information came from and which important facts remain missing.

This makes it easier to see later whether the outcome changed because an assumption was wrong or because conditions changed.

4. Record the realistic alternatives

List the options that were seriously considered.

Include the option of delaying, testing, reducing the commitment, or choosing not to act when those are realistic alternatives.

The journal does not need every imaginable possibility. It needs the alternatives that influenced the final choice.

5. State the tradeoffs and risks

Write down what is gained and what is given up under the selected option.

Identify the most important risks, warning signs, and conditions that could make the decision fail.

This creates a monitoring plan rather than treating risk as a vague concern.

6. Record the expected outcome

Describe what you expect to happen and the timeframe in which evidence should appear.

Include a measurable result when appropriate, but also record qualitative expectations such as improved clarity, reduced conflict, or increased flexibility.

Avoid making the prediction more precise than the available information allows.

7. Rate confidence and uncertainty

Record how confident you are and why.

Name the uncertainties that could materially change the result.

A confidence estimate makes it easier to evaluate calibration over time rather than simply labeling past decisions right or wrong.

8. Set a review date

Choose when the decision should be evaluated.

Some decisions produce quick evidence. Others require weeks or months before a fair review is possible.

A scheduled review prevents important lessons from being lost after attention moves elsewhere.

9. Review the outcome honestly

Compare the actual result with the expected result.

Identify which facts, assumptions, risks, and tradeoffs mattered most.

Determine what should be repeated, changed, or monitored in future decisions.
