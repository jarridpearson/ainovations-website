The part nobody tells you

People look you up before they call you. What they find is the whole decision.

Not a maybe. Somebody hears your name at a job site, in a waiting room, from a neighbor over a fence. Before they dial, they type it into their phone. That search is happening right now, today, whether or not you have anything waiting at the other end of it.

What they're actually checking

Three things, every time, in about eight seconds:

Are these people real? Do they do the thing I need? How do I reach them?

That's the entire test. It isn't about design awards or animations. It's about a stranger deciding, quickly, whether you're worth a phone call — and there is no version of that decision where finding nothing helps you.

What happens when there's nothing there

You don't get a rejection. You get silence.

Nobody calls to say they couldn't find you. They just call the next name on the list — the one who had a page that answered the question. You never learn that it happened, which is exactly what makes it so easy to keep ignoring. The lost job doesn't show up anywhere you'd look for it.

A Facebook page is somebody else's building

It works until the reach changes, the rules change, or the account gets locked and there's no one to call. You're renting an address you can be evicted from, and everything you've posted goes with it. Your own domain is the one address on the internet that stays yours.

A Google listing is a directory entry, not a business

It's a good thing to have and it isn't a website. It gives someone your hours and a map pin. It doesn't explain what you actually do, show what you've built, tell them what makes you the right call, or give them a real email address that ends in your own name instead of a free provider's.

The honest version of the pitch

A website will not make your phone ring on its own. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What it does is stop you losing the calls you were already going to get — the ones that started with somebody hearing your name and wanting to check. That's a smaller promise than most people make, and it's the true one. At $50 a month, it also doesn't have to work very hard to be worth it.

Straight answers

The questions we actually get.

Who owns the domain?

We register and hold it as part of the service, the same way we hold the hosting. If you ever want it moved to your own account — you leave, you hire somebody else, you just want it in your name — we transfer it and charge you only what the transfer actually costs. Not a penny of markup and no exit fee. Your name is never held hostage here.

What if I already have a domain?

Good — we'll point it at the new site. Nothing to buy and nothing to move.

How hard is the page I update?

It's a box and a Save button. If you can write a text message you can do it, and we'll walk you through it once on the day we hand it over.

What if I don't want to update anything?

Then don't. The site works perfectly well sitting still — the page just keeps whatever it last said. It's there for the days something changes, not as homework.

Does the AI make things up?

It answers from the documents you give us and it's built to say it doesn't know rather than invent an answer. Give it your real prices and it quotes your real prices. Give it nothing about warranties and it will tell someone to call you.

Why is the setup fee waived on three years?

Because the build is the expensive part for us and a longer commitment pays for it. If you're confident you're staying, that's worth $100 to you and worth more than $100 to us.

Can I move up to Rent-a-Site later?

Any time, and nothing gets rebuilt. It's the same site — we just start doing the work on it instead of leaving it to you.

Will this get me to the top of Google?

No, and be careful with anyone who promises it. We set the technical basics up correctly so you're findable for your own name and what you do. Fighting for competitive search terms is ongoing work, and that's Rent-a-Site.
