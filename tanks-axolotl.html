Axolotl Tank Setup: A Beginner's Guide to Getting It Right

Axolotls are one of the most charming animals you can keep at home, with those feathery gills and that permanent little smile. But here's the thing that trips up almost every new keeper: an axolotl is not a fish. It's an aquatic salamander from the cool, high-altitude waters of Mexico, and it needs a very different setup than the tropical community tank you might have pictured. Get the temperature and water chemistry right, and axolotls are genuinely rewarding, long-lived pets. Get them wrong, and they suffer fast.

This guide walks through an accurate axolotl tank setup, busts the common myths, and shows how to keep your water dialed in over the long haul.

The Essentials: Temperature and Cycling Come First

If you remember nothing else, remember these two things.

Axolotls are cold-water animals. Their ideal range is roughly 60–64°F (16–18°C), and they should never sit above about 72–74°F for any length of time. Heat is the number one killer of pet axolotls. Warm water stresses them, suppresses their appetite and immune system, and can be fatal. That means no aquarium heater — the opposite of most setups. In many homes, especially in summer, you'll need a clip-on fan across the water surface or an aquarium chiller to hold that cool target. Check your tank's actual temperature with a thermometer before you ever bring an axolotl home.

Your tank must be fully cycled. Axolotls have permeable skin and exposed external gills, which makes them extremely sensitive to nitrogenous waste. Before an axolotl moves in, your tank needs an established nitrogen cycle so that ammonia stays at 0 ppm and nitrite stays at 0 ppm. A fishless cycle typically takes several weeks — start it well ahead of time. Adding an axolotl to an uncycled tank is one of the most common (and most preventable) mistakes new keepers make.

Beyond those two, here's what a solid axolotl water parameters target looks like:

  • Temperature: 60–64°F (16–18°C)
  • Ammonia: 0 ppm
  • Nitrite: 0 ppm
  • Nitrate: low — keep it down with regular partial water changes
  • pH: around 7.4–7.6
  • GH/KH: moderate hardness; axolotls actually prefer water that isn't too soft

A few more setup fundamentals:

  • Tank size: a minimum of about 20 gallons for a single adult, and more if you plan to keep two. Axolotls are messy eaters and produce a lot of waste, so bigger is genuinely better.
  • Substrate: this is a big one. Avoid fine gravel — axolotls feed by suction and will swallow small stones, leading to impaction, a serious and sometimes fatal blockage. Safe choices are a bare-bottom tank (easiest to clean) or fine, soft aquarium sand that passes through them harmlessly. Large, smooth slate tiles are another option. When in doubt, bare bottom is the safest call.
  • Gentle flow: axolotls dislike strong current. Use a filter with an adjustable or baffled output so the water stays calm. A canister or sponge filter with the flow turned down works well.
  • Cover and hides: provide caves or PVC pipe for security, and keep a lid on — axolotls can and do try to climb out.

Axolotl Care FAQ

Can axolotls live with fish?

It's best not to. Most fish are tropical and need warm water that would harm an axolotl, and even cool-water fish tend to nip at those tempting external gills. On top of that, an axolotl may try to eat small fish and risk impaction or injury. The safest tank is an axolotl kept alone, or with another axolotl of the same size only.

Do axolotls need a heater?

No — the opposite. Axolotls need cool water (60–64°F). A heater is dangerous for them. If your home runs warm, use a fan or chiller to bring the temperature down, not up.

What's the safest substrate for an axolotl tank?

Bare bottom is the safest, easiest-to-clean option. Fine, soft aquarium sand is also widely accepted. Avoid gravel and any small stones an axolotl could swallow, since impaction is a real risk.

How big should an axolotl tank be?

Plan for at least 20 gallons for one adult, and scale up for a second. Axolotls produce a lot of waste, so extra water volume helps keep your parameters stable.

What do axolotls eat?

A staple diet of earthworms and nightcrawlers is excellent, supplemented with sinking soft pellets made for axolotls. Feed appropriately sized food and remove uneaten bits to protect your water quality.
